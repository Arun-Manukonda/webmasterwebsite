AI all around you

Artificial intelligence (AI) is technology that lets computers do things we normally connect with human thinking: recognizing faces, understanding speech, predicting what comes next, and making decisions.

Educators often organize AI around five big ideas: computers perceive the world with sensors, represent what they know, learn from data, interact naturally with people, and affect society in good and bad ways.[6]

Try this: Before your next class, count how many AI-powered features you use. How many did you find?

Face unlock

Recognizes your face from patterns it learned.

Map apps

Predict traffic and pick faster routes.

Spam filters

Learn which emails look like junk.

Recommendations

Guess what video or song you'll want next.

Learning from examples

Old-school programs follow rules a person writes, step by step. Machine learning (ML) works differently: we give the computer many examples (data), often with the right answers (labels), and it figures out the patterns itself.[7]

  • Supervised learning: labeled examples, such as "photo → cat."
  • Unsupervised learning: the computer finds groups on its own.
  • Reinforcement learning: it learns from rewards, like points in a game.

Key idea: a model is only as good as its examples. Too few, too similar, or unfair examples mean bad predictions. You'll see this yourself in the challenge below.

Inside a neural network

Many modern AI systems use neural networks, layers of tiny math units loosely inspired by brain cells. Each connection has a weight that controls how much it matters.

Training works like practice: the network guesses, checks how wrong it was, and nudges its weights to do a little better. After millions of rounds, it becomes skilled at the task. Networks with many layers are called deep learning.[8]

Why it matters: no one hand-writes those millions of weights, so even experts can't always explain exactly why a network made a choice.

How chatbots write

Chatbots run on large language models (LLMs). These are giant neural networks trained on huge amounts of text to do one thing extremely well: predict the next token (a word or piece of a word).

The model writes an answer by predicting one token, adding it, and predicting again. That's why answers sound fluent. But "sounds right" is not the same as "is right." When the patterns point to something plausible but false, the model can hallucinate facts, quotes, or sources.

Remember: an LLM is a prediction engine, not a fact database. You'll practice catching its mistakes on Trail 2.

Challenge

Teach the Machine

+50 XP

You're training a nearest-neighbor classifier, one of the simplest machine learning models. Add example fruits to the chart. The model shades the regions where it would guess "apple" or "lime." Then drop mystery fruits and see what it predicts. It looks at the 3 closest examples and takes a vote.

Experiment: Put one lime in the "apple" corner, then test a fruit nearby. See how one odd example can change predictions? That's how messy or biased data leads to mistakes.
