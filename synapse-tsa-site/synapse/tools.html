Lesson 1

The student AI toolbox

"AI" isn't one app. It's a whole toolbox. Different tools are built for different jobs, and each has its own strengths and risks.

Type of toolGreat forWatch out for
Chat assistants
e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot		Explaining concepts, brainstorming, practice questions, feedback on your ideasConfident mistakes; doing the work for you instead of with you
AI search & research toolsSummarizing sources and linking to themSummaries that misread the source; always open the links
Writing assistants
grammar and clarity checkers		Catching typos and awkward sentencesSome teachers count these as AI help, so ask first
AI tutors
built into learning platforms		Step-by-step hints without giving away answersStill need to check explanations against class notes
Image & media generatorsVisual brainstorming, mock-ups for projectsCopyright questions, deepfakes, disclosure rules
Accessibility toolsSpeech-to-text, live captions, translation, read-aloudAccuracy on names and technical terms

Product names are examples only, not endorsements. Tools change quickly.

Before you use any tool

Check your teacher's AI policy for that assignment, and check the tool's terms. Many AI services have minimum ages or require a parent's permission for users under 18.

Lesson 2

Writing prompts that work

A prompt is the instruction you give an AI. Vague prompts get vague answers. Use the R-T-C-F formula to make every prompt specific:

R: Role

Who should the AI act as? A tutor, a coach, a debate partner?

T: Task

What exactly do you want it to do?

C: Context

What does it need to know about you, your class, or your goal?

F: Format

How should the answer look? A list, a table, a quiz, 100 words?

Here's a prompt broken into its parts:

Act as a patient chemistry tutor. Explain the difference between ionic and covalent bonds. I'm in 10th grade and I understand what electrons are, but bonding confuses me. Use a simple analogy, then give me a 3-row comparison table.

RoleTaskContextFormat
Weak

explain bonds

Could mean chemistry, finance, or friendship. No level, no format.

Strong

Act as a chemistry tutor. Explain ionic vs. covalent bonds for a 10th grader, using an analogy and a short table.

Iterate: the first answer is a first draft

Keep the conversation going. Try follow-ups like:

  • "Make that simpler" or "Go one level deeper."
  • "Give me a different example related to sports."
  • "What's a common misconception about this?"
  • "What parts of your answer might be wrong or oversimplified?"
Lesson 3

AI as a study partner

The best way to use AI for school is to make it help you think, not think for you. Here are six study moves with prompts you can borrow:

Explain it another way

I read my textbook section on mitosis but I'm lost. Explain it using an analogy about a factory.

Quiz me

Ask me 5 questions about the causes of WWI, one at a time. Wait for my answer and tell me what I missed.

Be a Socratic tutor

Help me solve this algebra problem, but don't give me the answer. Ask me guiding questions instead.

Feedback, not ghostwriting

Here's my thesis statement. Point out what's unclear, but don't rewrite it for me.

Plan my studying

I have a biology test Friday and 45 minutes a night. Make a 4-day study plan.

Turn notes into flashcards

Turn these class notes into 10 question-and-answer flashcards: [paste your own notes]

The "who did the thinking?" test

After using AI, ask yourself: could I explain this to a friend without the screen in front of me? If yes, AI helped you learn. If no, it just did the work.

Lesson 4

Verify before you trust

Because AI can hallucinate, checking its work is a core skill, not an extra step. A popular fact-checking method from digital-literacy educators is SIFT:

S: Stop

Pause before you believe or share. Does this seem too neat, too surprising, or too convenient?

I: Investigate the source

Who is behind the information? AI isn't a source itself; find where the claim comes from.

F: Find better coverage

Search for the claim in trusted sources: textbooks, .gov and .edu sites, reputable news.

T: Trace claims

Follow quotes, numbers, and studies back to the original to see if they're accurate and in context.

AI-specific checklist

  • Citations: search for every source the AI gives you. Confirm it exists and says what the AI claims.
  • Numbers and dates: confirm with at least two reliable sources.
  • Recent events: the AI may not know about them, so check the date of your information.
  • Your class materials win: if AI disagrees with your teacher or textbook, ask your teacher.
