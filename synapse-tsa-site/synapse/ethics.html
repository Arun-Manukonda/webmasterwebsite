Lesson 1

Academic integrity in the age of AI

Academic integrity means the work you turn in honestly shows what you know and can do. AI doesn't change that principle. It just adds new situations to think about.

Every class can have a different AI policy, and policies can change from one assignment to the next. A helpful way to think about it is a traffic light:

SignalWhat it usually looks like
Green: usually OKAsking AI to explain a concept, quizzing yourself, planning study time, brainstorming when allowed
Yellow: ask firstGrammar or rewording tools on graded writing, AI-generated images in a project, getting feedback on a draft
Red: not OKSubmitting AI-written work as your own, using AI on tests, using AI when the teacher said no
Three habits that keep you safe

1. Know the policy (and ask when it's unclear). 2. Disclose any AI you used. 3. Keep your notes and drafts so you can show your process.

Lesson 2

Bias and fairness

AI systems learn from data created by people, and that data reflects the real world, including its unfairness. Bias can sneak in at three points:

The data

Some groups are under-represented, or the data records unfair past decisions.

The design

Builders choose what the system measures and optimizes, and those choices have effects.

The use

A tool built for one purpose gets used for another it wasn't tested for.

Real-world examples

  • A 2019 study by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) tested face recognition algorithms and found that many of them made more errors on some demographic groups than others.
  • In 2018, news reports described an experimental hiring tool at a large tech company that was scrapped after it learned to favor men's résumés, because it was trained on past hiring patterns.
What you can do

Question outputs that stereotype people, ask the AI for multiple perspectives, compare with diverse human sources, and speak up when a tool seems unfair.

Lesson 3

Privacy and safety

When you type into an AI tool, that information goes to a company's servers. It may be stored, reviewed, or used to improve the product, depending on the tool and your settings.

Keep out of AI tools

Full name with address, phone number, passwords, student ID numbers, health details, and anyone else's private information or photos.

Safer to share

General questions, your own class notes (without personal info), and practice problems.

Deepfakes and consent

A deepfake is AI-generated or AI-altered media that realistically shows someone saying or doing something they never did. Creating or sharing fake images of real people, especially classmates, can cause serious harm, can count as harassment, and in many places is against the law.

Quick rule

If you wouldn't post it on a public bulletin board, don't paste it into a chatbot. If someone didn't agree to it, don't generate it.

Lesson 4

Being a responsible AI citizen

Disclose and cite

When AI is allowed, be transparent about how you used it. Many teachers ask for a short disclosure statement:

AI use statement: I used [tool name] to brainstorm possible topics and to quiz myself on key terms. All research, writing, and final decisions are my own.

Style guides such as MLA and APA have published guidance for citing generative AI. An MLA-style citation generally includes the prompt, the tool's name, the company, the date, and the web address:

"Explain the main causes of the French Revolution in simple terms" prompt. [Tool name], [Company], [Day Month Year], [web address].

Think beyond yourself

  • Misinformation: AI makes it easy to create convincing fake content. Verify before you share.
  • Environment: training and running large AI models uses significant electricity and water in data centers. Use AI with purpose.
  • Human in the loop: people should review AI output and stay accountable, especially for decisions about other people.
  • Your future: nearly every career, from medicine to marketing, is being affected by AI. The students who learn to use it thoughtfully will lead.
